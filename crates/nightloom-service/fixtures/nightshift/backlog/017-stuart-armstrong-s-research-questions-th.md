##### §J.1 — on new Q2 (old 5): what humans are doing when they notice OOD

Verbatim:

> There's a lot we don't know about this, but here's what I've been able to gather:
> there seem to be at least two different modes of evaluation within the brain, and
> they generalize differently (which sounds to me like their most important
> distinction). There's some behavioral evidence for this in animals
> (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376635710000033, Adams &
> Dickinson, 1981), providing evidence for there being a goal-directed system and a
> habitual system (perhaps the cognitive equivalent of Daniel Kahneman's System 1
> and System 2 thinking?). There's also been more recent research
> (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21435563/) demonstrating this in humans, showing
> how human behavior tends to reflect a hybrid between both model free and model
> based, and other experiments have seemed to suggest that both evaluators compute
> answers simultaneously. As a result, there is some method of combining these
> results for causing action. Each of these systems are paired with some weighting,
> representing how certain they are about their predictions. These are combined in
> order to decide which evaluator controls the action.
>
> 1. Lee et al. propose a three-tiered structure: in the first, the model-free
>    system generates a reward prediction error (RPE), and the model-based system
>    generates a State Prediction Error (SPE). Second tier calculates a dynamic,
>    running metric of reliability. The third tier is the arbitrator, which takes in
>    the reliability metrics and produces a weighted sum, signifying the answer.
>
> One moral viewpoint of this from Cushman 2013
> (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1088868313495594) poses that
> action-based moral judgement maps onto a model-free reinforcement learning, such
> that the brain assigns value to action based on a history of (social/evolutionary)
> reinforcement, without explicitly representing downstream consequences. This might
> govern our reflexes, or our strong aversion to things that, to our brain, seem
> like they would be harmful (e.g., a strong aversion to shooting a fake gun). The
> second is an outcome-based valuation (model-based), in which the brain contains a
> causal model of the world, and uses it to simulate consequences. This process is
> much more computationally expensive. Interestingly enough, the first can be
> considered the deontological perspective, and the second is the utilitarian.

##### §J.2 — on new Q5 (old 8): ACE-like divergence without unlabelled data

Verbatim:

> Perhaps an interesting thought is to think about this idea more broadly: can we
> find a principled basis set of generalization mechanisms, such that given one
> evaluator per mechanism, the ensemble covers the set of ways in which
> generalization can fail?
>
> Maybe we could utilize an architectural disagreement here. it seems like the
> brain's diversity of evaluators comes from architectural differences, or
> structurally different learners, rather than random seeds or unlabelled data.
> Perhaps we could force architectural diversity, such that to begin with, one
> evaluator generalizes by cached case similarity (model free) and one that
> generalizes by explicit reasoning (model based), and read disagreement between
> them.
>
> In a stable environment, both a model-free and model-based evaluator should
> provide the same answers: the cache is filled by experience that the model also
> explains. However, when they diverge these two architectures diverge from one
> another for a more principled reason. The cache generalizes by similarity to
> remembered situations and the planner generalizes by recomputation from structure.
> There's been existing research (Lee, Shimojo, O'Doherty) attempting to have a
> third signal weigh these two evaluators by how reliable each has recently been
> measured by their own prediction errors.
>
> One potential question is why specifically use these two architectures and not
> some any other arbitrary pair. For starters, I think one potential reason is that
> it seems that these two are two extremes of a single axis, which is how much does
> an agent recompute versus from structure versus reuse a stored answer. Another
> potential reason is that it seems these two evaluator methods have uncorrelated
> modes of failure. Cache failures seem to occur when there is a situation that
> looks like a cache situation but is not and therefore triggers on surface
> similarity otherwise known as the spurious correlation failure. On the other hand,
> planner failures are more about the fundamental model of how things work being
> wrong. Because ACE's heads share an architecture and an optimizer, they differ
> only in which feature they picked. So their modes of failure are the same kind,
> and only the disagreement objective keeps them apart.
>
> Literature
>
> Pieces of the puzzle have already been tested: hyper deep ensembles (Wenzel et al.
> 2020) vary hyperparameters and architectures, extending standard deep ensembles to
> intentionally generate a more diverse set of models, which are more resilient to
> dataset shift. Similarly, others like AutoDEUQ (Egele et al. 2022) searches
> architectures for ensemble diversity, and architecturally diverse ensembles being
> used as an uncertainty metric for atomistic models. However, these approaches seem
> to miss two fundamental elements.
>
> 1. Firstly, they optimize for algorithmic or architectural diversity, which
>    sometimes implies uncorrelated failure modes, but doesn't guarantee it. To
>    select for truly uncorrelated failure modes, one would have to take an objective
>    function that explicitly penalizes models for making the same mistakes
>    out-of-distribution, rather than hoping that emerges as a byproduct of
>    architectural diversity.
> 2. Second, these approaches don't seem to exist within a value domain. They
>    primarily remain on the statistical or physical level, with directly measurable
>    variance reduction-esque methods. By contrast, the value domain provides a very
>    different rubric with which error should be reasoned about.
>
> Plan2Explore (Pathak et al., 2019) trains an ensemble to predict future
> environmental states, and rewards an agent for exploring locations for which the
> ensemble contains higher disagreement. However, the architectures themselves are
> homogenous, and therefore may share the same blind spots.
>
> The reason I'd bet this hasn't been done, is because finding failure mode
> independence systematically through mechanistic differences is not solved already.
>
> So possibly, if we find a way to find some sort of a principled basis set of
> generalization mechanisms, we could utilize their disagreement the same way Lee et
> al. seems to show that the human brain does. So how can we find this principled
> basis set?
>
> Our central question is, we're trying to determine how to find a set of
> generalization mechanisms such that each one contributes a fundamentally different
> mode of failure. If we can accomplish this, an ensemble with the variety of
> architectures that contribute to these generalization mechanisms can utilize its
> internal disagreement as a signal for value underdetermination or incorrect
> generalization.

> ## Background
>
> **Value underdetermination** is a label for a scenario in which a true value and
> its proxy cleanly overlap in the training distribution, yet split in deployment.
> When an actor encounters such a situation in deployment, it confidently acts in
> accordance with the proxy it was trained for, disregarding the true value that
> developers thought was properly instilled through the proxy. In its own reading,
> the agent is both confident and correct to be confident.
>
> Sycophancy is broadly an example of value underdetermination in LLMs. Models are
> trained to be sincerely helpful, and developers use human rating as the proxy for
> this true value. These are rather indistinguishable across nearly all preference
> data, because a truly helpful answer is precisely what a rater would approve of.
> The split, however, shows up out of distribution, on cases where models from many
> frontier labs wrongly admit mistakes, give biased feedback, and mimic user errors.
>
> Therefore, mitigating the harmful effects of value underdetermination is a
> necessary prerequisite to being able to trust LLMs or authorize their access to
> more infrastructure, resources, and valuable credentials.
>
> ## Design
>
> Existing approaches to LLM post-training alignment fall short in practice because
> they are unable to detect value underdetermination. I propose a new method, which
> amalgamates numerous existing (but separately built) frameworks into a
> comprehensive training and deployment method that increases value alignment in
> LLMs.
>
> Essentially, rather than training a model to converge on one value rule, the
> training process develops many competing hypotheses for what the desired value
> could be and stores them in an explicit library of natural language principles
> that the agent carries, each assigned a *weight*. In familiar environments, highly
> weighted principles agree on their prescribed action and the agent acts according
> to them. On novel cases where the principles conflict, that disagreement is the
> warning signal the agent can use at the time of action.
>
> Utilizing disagreement as a signal accomplishes two tasks.
>
> In training, disagreement identifies which cases are worth a human's time; a
> human panel answers and that answer is generalized into a vast set of candidate
> principles. These principles are then scored against every case seen so far during
> training, allowing them to be ranked and given learned *weights*. Over many
> unrelated cases, accumulated evidence pulls apart correlations that no single case
> could resolve. A separate training process occurs afterwards in order to develop a
> library of *meta-principles*, which act over other principles (rather than the
> environment directly) and arbitrate the disagreements that are answerable rather
> than underdetermined.
>
> In deployment, disagreement is the trigger to defer to a human rather than
> guessing based on a proxy, allowing for the model to detect and abstain once its
> values are underdetermined.
>
> Because the principles are in natural language, they are readable, auditable, and
> editable by developers, they provide significantly more interpretability than
> current alignment techniques. And unlike Constitutional AI, which collapses the
> pluralistic and immensely vast range of human values into one single set curated
> by humans rather than learned by training itself, this framework allows for a
> pluralistic approach to value alignment, while also employing disagreement during
> deployment in a way that constitutional approaches cannot.
>
> Finally, this approach allows the agent to *continually learn* values in a novel
> way: upon deferring at deployment, the environment and the human's eventual answer
> can then be added into the training loop as a new case to build the model's moral
> robustness, making training a continual and never-ending process rather than
> stagnated.
